THE CENTRAL QUESTION

One decision.
Many different outcomes.

What is the difference between wanting to donate, being registered, and actually becoming an organ donor? This story follows three strands: participation over time , differences between states , and the role of family consent .

A quick definition: new registrations count sign-ups in a year; registration rate is the proportion of people aged 16+ registered; deceased donors are people whose organs were donated after death; transplant recipients are people who received organs. These are not interchangeable counts.

For readers in Malaysia: Australia offers a useful case study of how registrations, family decisions and actual transplants are different. All figures below describe Australia; they should not be assumed to represent Malaysia.

When families are asked

The hospital conversation and medical eligibility affect whether donation proceeds.

In 2025, roughly 1,670 people died in circumstances where donation could be considered. Only 557 became deceased organ donors. This does not mean 1,113 potential donors simply declined.

WHAT THE DATA CANNOT TELL US

Numbers are not decisions.
People are.

Registration helps communicate a person's wishes, but not everyone who registers will be able to donate. Families, clinicians and medical eligibility are all involved. The 2025 national data shows that people can make their intentions clearer even when an eventual transplant is not guaranteed.

We should not infer that state differences are caused by awareness, culture, healthcare quality, or policy from these charts alone. Such claims need additional evidence.

DEFINITIONS

New registrations are annual sign-ups. Registration rate is the proportion of residents aged 16+ who are registered. Deceased donors are people whose organs were donated after death. Family consent rate is the share of families who agreed to donation in the reported year.
